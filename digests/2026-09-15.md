## Daniel Pink

**[Remote Work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HFiHlOcwuRM)**
Economists John Barrios, Jessie Guo, and Yanping Zhu sort firms by how easily their work could go remote and find that financial misconduct fell by roughly half at the firms best suited to remote work after 2020 — reframing distance not as a productivity variable but as an organizational integrity one.

- **Fraud is a team sport.** The effect was strongest at the most collaborative firms, which is the tell: coordinating misconduct depends on informal, in-person contact. Distance raised the cost of keeping a conspiracy together.
- **Collaborative settings showed the largest drop.** Firms where in-person coordination had been easiest saw the biggest reduction in misconduct once that coordination was cut — the environments with the most to lose from distance were also the ones where distance did the most integrity work.
- **One number to hold onto.** Financial misconduct fell by roughly half at firms best positioned for remote work, a magnitude large enough to take seriously as a governance consideration, not just an anecdote.

## AI Engineer

**[Every step you take, every call you make: the reliable agent stack — Giselle van Dongen, Restate](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cI7zfqusmFU)**
Giselle van Dongen argues that the fragile part of production agent systems is not the model loop — it is the infrastructure underneath it: retry logic, recovery logic, and session state that teams keep rebuilding badly. Restate's durable execution model is her proposed alternative, illustrated by an agent that suspends a promise waiting for human approval for up to a month, spanning restarts and redeploys, while consuming zero serverless execution time during the wait.

- **The suspended promise as the sharpest demo.** A human-in-the-loop approval step that can wait a month without tying up compute is an immediately legible argument for durable execution over hand-rolled retry and recovery stacks.
- **Reliability infrastructure is the hidden rebuild tax.** Van Dongen's core claim: every team rebuilds this layer poorly. The real cost of unreliable agents is not in the model loop itself but in the glue that makes agents survive failures, restarts, and redeploys.
- **Serverless economics change the calculus.** Zero execution time during a wait state means suspended durable workflows are not just architecturally cleaner — they are also cheaper than keeping a long-running process alive through an open-ended human approval window.
- **The talk skips the agent loop deliberately.** Targeting the infrastructure layer rather than agent design is a signal about where van Dongen believes production systems are most consistently underbuilt.

**[Loophole: Adversarial Agents To Stress Test Your Morality — Brendan Rappazzo, Morgan Stanley](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hOWU0KPUp1k)**
Brendan Rappazzo built Loophole — an open source project he is explicit is personal work, unrelated to his employer — to surface the gap between what a written moral code permits and what it was meant to prevent. The motivating case: an insurance company that trains its risk model not on DNA but on artifacts derived from DNA stays within the letter of the user's stated moral code while violating its intent.

- **The artifact loophole is a real attack surface.** Training on derived data rather than the original source is a technique for sidestepping stated policy while remaining technically compliant. Loophole is built to find these structurally legal but morally violating patterns before deployment.
- **Adversarial agents as moral penetration testing.** The methodology treats moral code enforcement the way security teams treat pentesting: purpose-built adversarial agents probe for gaps before bad actors or edge cases expose them in production.
- **The project started personal.** Rappazzo's own DNA submission to a consumer ancestry service prompted the work — a grounding detail that makes clear the risks of moral-code gaps are already operating at consumer scale, not just in enterprise edge cases.
- **Employer independence matters for adoption.** The explicit disavowal of Morgan Stanley involvement removes licensing and constraint ambiguity for organizations evaluating Loophole for internal use.

**[Harness Engineering: Building the Production Cage for Powerful Domain Agents — Mike Chambers, AWS](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gxVZ_1tuuq4)**
Mike Chambers defines a harness as everything in an agent minus the model — remove the model, and what remains is the harness. That subtraction definition lets him split agent infrastructure into two distinct problem sets: agents people use, and agents organizations build and deploy into production.

- **The subtraction definition forces an explicit inventory.** Identifying the harness by removing the model compels teams to enumerate what is left: memory, skills, tools, MCP servers, orchestration, and guardrails — all the parts that tend to be left implicit until something breaks.
- **Two categories, two different harnesses.** Agents-we-use (coding assistants, productivity tools) have harnesses centered on memory, skills, and tool access. Agents-we-build have harnesses centered on orchestration, deployment constraints, and the controls that constitute the production cage.
- **The cage framing is about oversight, not restriction.** Chambers opens with the dictionary definition — a harness is straps and fastenings used to control an animal — to make the point that a production cage for a powerful domain agent is how an organization maintains meaningful oversight of what the model does on its behalf.

**[Tokens Should Have Jobs — Katelyn Lesse & Angela Jiang, Anthropic](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PXj0p_mW9nI)**
Katelyn Lesse and Angela Jiang challenge the assumption buried in most agent tuning: that tokens are fungible, making count the only lever on quality. Given an identical budget of roughly 600,000 tokens, an execution-only agent scored 76 on a financial analysis benchmark; an agent that spent part of that same budget consulting a second agent for advice scored 89.

- **Same tokens, different jobs, 13-point lift.** The delta between 76 and 89 did not come from adding tokens — it came from redirecting some execution tokens into an advisor role. That is a structural argument against treating all tokens as interchangeable compute.
- **Token roles as an explicit design dimension.** The talk reframes agent architecture: rather than asking "how many tokens?", the productive question is "what job does each token do?" — execution, verification, synthesis, or consultation.
- **Specialization beats concentration.** Splitting the budget between an executor and an advisor produced better answers than concentrating the same budget on execution alone, suggesting role differentiation matters even within a fixed resource envelope.
- **The observation comes from production breadth.** Lesse leads platform engineering and Jiang leads platform product at Anthropic — their framing reflects how teams across many real deployments actually allocate token budgets, not a controlled lab result.
