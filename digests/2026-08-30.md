## AWS What's New

**[Amazon EC2 C8gn instances are now available in AWS Europe (Paris) region](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/08/amazon-ec2-c8gn-europe-paris/)**
Graviton4-powered C8gn instances expand to the AWS Europe (Paris) region, offering up to 30% better compute performance than Graviton3-based C7gn instances. The instances feature 6th-generation AWS Nitro Cards and deliver up to 600 Gbps network bandwidth — the highest among network-optimized EC2 instances.

**[Amazon Bedrock AgentCore Memory now supports fine-grained access control](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/08/agentcorememory-fine-grained-access-control)**
AgentCore Memory adds per-user and per-tenant memory isolation via Cedar policies attached to an AgentCore Gateway configured for OAuth (JWT) authentication, removing the need to build custom authorization logic. Developers can enforce that each authenticated caller accesses only their own actor's data, scoped through an existing Gateway without writing bespoke access-control code.

**[Amazon Bedrock AgentCore Memory now supports flexible namespace variables](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/08/agentcorememory-flexible-namespaces)**
Developers can now define custom namespace variables to scope long-term memories along any application-specific dimension — such as organization, tenant, team, or environment — without creating duplicate strategies or overloading built-in variables. This gives multi-tenant and complex-hierarchy applications fine-grained control over how memories are organized, isolated, and accessed.

## AI Engineer

**[The Half Life of Agent Infrastructure — Ben Kus, Box](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sM1iYgz93HI)**
Ben Kus opens by publicly retracting graph-based agent architecture advice he gave at the same conference a year prior — not because it was wrong at the time, but because a better approach arrived, as it did for most of what was presented that year. He runs the same exercise across models, agent design, and retrieval, finding the same short cycle everywhere: practitioner consensus has a half-life, and last year's confident recommendation is this year's liability candidate.

- **Retraction is the practitioner signal.** Kus chose to walk back the advice on the same stage where he gave it. The point is not that graph-based agents were wrong; the point is that the field moves fast enough that acting on one year's architectural consensus without questioning it compounds technical debt silently.
- **The half-life applies across the full stack, not just topology.** He applies the "what changed" lens to models, agent design, and retrieval — each shows the same cycling dynamic. Any single component that felt settled twelve months ago is worth re-examining now.
- **Timing matters as much as correctness.** The attendee who implemented the graph-based approach immediately after the prior conference may have locked in a pattern that was already being superseded by the time they shipped. Speed of action on conference advice is not always an advantage.
- **"Nothing about the advice was wrong when he gave it."** That precise framing distinguishes a fast-moving field from a field where practitioners give bad advice. The implication for infrastructure decisions: document the context and constraints that made a choice correct, so the trigger for revisiting it is clear.

**[Which AI startups actually land enterprise contracts? — Brian Lewis, Millennium](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7A65O-0lvKE)**
Brian Lewis, speaking personally from the buy side at a hedge fund, maps the full enterprise evaluation funnel: roughly 10-15 startups considered per internal pain point, 2-3 demos booked, zero or one pilot run, and approximately one contract signed per four pilots — about 5% of demo calls reaching a signature, consistent with published benchmarks. The talk is a detailed catalogue of where the other 95% of deals die, with examples specific enough to be uncomfortable.

- **The 5% close rate is structural, not a streak of bad luck.** Lewis notes his figure matches published benchmarks, which means the brutal funnel is the baseline, not an outlier. Startups should plan for it rather than assume a superior product will outperform it.
- **Pain-point precision is the first filter, before any demo.** Evaluating 10-15 startups per pain point means most are eliminated without ever getting a call. Startups that cannot map tightly to a precisely defined internal problem are cut at the discovery stage.
- **The demo-to-pilot gap is where most deals die.** Running zero or one pilot per two or three demos means a significant fraction of startups that reach a live evaluation still do not convert. A compelling demo does not predict a successful pilot.
- **One contract per four pilots sets the close-rate ceiling.** Even after a startup survives to a pilot, the conversion rate is not close to one. The implication is that for most enterprise AI startups, the sales cycle consumes far more resources than founders model early on.
- **Speaking personally enabled the specificity.** The explicit disclaimer that Lewis speaks for himself rather than his employer is what makes the concrete failure-mode examples possible — and what makes the talk more useful than generic enterprise sales advice.
