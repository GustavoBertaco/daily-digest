## Daniel Pink

**[Horse Spinning](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nIcN81-lqWs)**
Drawing on Thomas Toppino's research at Villanova, Daniel Pink demonstrates that reversible figures — ambiguous images the brain can flip between two interpretations — can be deliberately controlled by choosing which feature to attend to first, then uses this as a concrete lens for the distinction between perspective-taking and empathy.

- **Attention direction controls perception.** Toppino's work (Perception & Psychophysics, 2003) shows that intentional control of which interpretation you see is achievable by selecting where to look first — staring at the feet of a spinning horse is a documented technique for flipping its perceived direction.
- **Perspective-taking and empathy are distinct cognitive operations.** Pink's formulation from *To Sell Is Human*: perspective-taking is cognitive (thinking about another's viewpoint), while empathy is emotional (feeling what they feel). The two are not interchangeable, and conflating them produces different mistakes in persuasion and negotiation.
- **Cognitive flexibility is trainable.** The ability to deliberately flip between interpretations of the same ambiguous stimulus is not purely passive perception; it can be directed through attention, which has practical implications for how people approach ambiguous situations and other people's perspectives.

## AI Engineer

**[Why Waiting for the Next AI Model Can Pay Off - John Stecher, Blackstone & Scott Worland, Norm AI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ra8X7_bnujw)**
Blackstone CTO John Stecher and Norm AI CTO Scott Worland converge on a counterintuitive thesis: patience on model adoption can compound returns, but only when the underlying data infrastructure and evaluation systems are solid first — and the decision calculus must include the explicit option value of waiting for the next model generation.

- **Data strategy and straight-through processing precede agents.** Stecher's sequence is deliberate: get the data foundation right, automate end-to-end workflows, then layer agents on top — not the reverse. Skipping the foundation steps creates compounding tech debt underneath agent systems.
- **ROI calculations must include the option to wait.** Stecher explicitly weighs build-now cost against holding for a better model; for some applications, the next model generation closes the accuracy gap that justifies the whole project, making waiting the rational choice.
- **Internal apps are now disposable.** A threshold has been crossed where rebuilding internal tooling from scratch with a newer model is faster and cheaper than extending legacy code, which changes how organizations should approach internal software investment.
- **Hand-labeled evals exposed workflow code as the bottleneck.** Norm AI found through a large hand-labeled eval set that its carefully crafted workflow orchestration was limiting performance — the complexity that looked like rigor was getting in the model's way.
- **Platform rebuilds should be driven by eval results.** Worland describes rebuilding Norm's platform after the eval revealed the real constraints — a measure-first, rebuild-second cycle that differs from the more common build-then-evaluate pattern.

**[How We Took an AI Agent From 41% to 97% Accurate - Milind Sunil Shah, Brainbase Labs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4VmugsE-iP0)**
Using a production Domino's competitor price-tracking agent as a case study, Milind Shah demonstrates that moving from 41% to 97% accuracy over eleven versions came entirely through improved instruction design, ontology work, and iterative evals — with no model change, cost per source halving from $2 to $1, and runtime dropping from 129 to 71 minutes.

- **Model swaps are rarely the fix.** The entire accuracy jump was achieved through better instructions, skill definitions, and playbooks — not by switching to a larger or newer model. This is the clearest documented case that prompt and playbook engineering compounds.
- **A six-step framework structures improvement.** Shah's sequence — kickoff, ontology, benchmarks and evals, a narrow first agent, iteration — separates domain definition from agent construction, keeping each stage's scope contained.
- **Ontology before code.** Defining the domain vocabulary (what constitutes a "competitor," a "price," a "source") early reduces ambiguity that otherwise propagates into every downstream prompt and evaluation.
- **Narrow scope first.** The first agent is intentionally limited; breadth is added only after the core loop is reliable, keeping the eval surface manageable during the most fragile early iterations.
- **One number to remember:** 41% to 97% accuracy across eleven versions, no model change — purely instruction and playbook engineering, with cost and runtime both cut roughly in half.

**[Stop Making Your Customers Click Approve - Emil Chetty, Wise](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V2UqEUA8NjM)**
Emil Chetty of Wise reframes the agentic UX problem: the interrupt-driven confirmation pattern has turned customers into an approval queue, and the fix is not better UX but better authorization architecture — where the customer defines the job once as a locked agreement, the agent selects its own steps, and existing payment infrastructure enforces the terms end-to-end.

- **The approval queue is a design failure.** Current AI assistants default to interrupt-driven confirmations, which signals the agent is not trusted to operate autonomously and degrades the user experience to something worse than the manual process it replaces.
- **Wise's regulatory context makes authorization proof non-negotiable.** Moving $243 billion across borders for nearly 19 million customers under more than 80 licenses means every payment must carry verifiable proof of what the customer authorized — a click in a chat interface is not sufficient evidence.
- **Lock the agreement, not the steps.** The customer authorizes the outcome once; the agent selects its own steps within that locked agreement; existing payment software enforces the terms, decoupling user consent from operational execution.
- **Existing enforcement infrastructure as the trust layer.** Rather than building new agent-specific guardrails, Wise routes agent actions through its existing payment software, which already enforces regulatory terms — meaning even a compromised or tricked agent cannot exceed what was originally authorized.

**[Our Smaller Model Beat Opus on Finance Tasks - Alexander Shevchenko, Ramp](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T-42NrYjwxM)**
Ramp Labs makes the case that application-layer companies should run applied research rather than wait for frontier model upgrades, demonstrating three production systems where custom work outperformed off-the-shelf models on the dimensions that matter for finance: accuracy, latency, and cost.

- **Post-training domain-specific models is viable at the application layer.** A Qwen 3.5 model post-trained on financial data beat Opus 4.6 on financial question answering at Haiku-level latency — better task accuracy at lower inference cost.
- **KV cache "cartridges" compress context 20-30x.** Pre-packing agent context into KV cache cartridges lets agents for tasks like month-end close operate with far more relevant context than would fit at runtime, without the latency cost of stuffing long contexts into every call.
- **Agent swarms as a security primitive.** A swarm of 10,000 parallel agents pen-tested Ramp's own systems and found seven high-severity vulnerabilities — a novel application of parallel agentic work to offensive security at scale.
- **Applied research earns its keep when frontier model cadence doesn't match business cadence.** Shevchenko argues that the release cycle for frontier models does not align with the cycle of business needs, making in-house model work economically rational for companies with sufficient domain data and engineering capacity.

**[AI Labs Will Trap You or Replace You - Dillon Rolnick, Nous Research](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sg6vtKOAOQ0)**
Dillon Rolnick argues that as AI models commoditize, the labs that spent trillions building them have two extraction paths: lock enterprise customers into their platforms and raise prices, or deploy their own systems to compete directly in high-margin verticals like finance — leaving builders caught between both risks unless they build on open foundations they control.

- **Labs have two paths to recoup trillion-dollar investments.** Lock in customers through platform dependency and raise prices over time, or build vertically-integrated products to compete directly in lucrative industries. Neither is neutral for enterprise buyers planning multi-year AI strategies.
- **Proprietary software moats are gone.** The same model capabilities that power any enterprise tool are accessible to anyone; competitive differentiation through code is no longer durable against a lab that can replicate the application layer with its own systems.
- **Agents are closer to operating systems than chatbots.** The framing matters for risk assessment: an OS-level dependency on a proprietary AI platform creates lock-in far more entrenched than a SaaS dependency, because it reaches into every workflow rather than one surface.
- **Open foundations are the counter-strategy.** Building on open-weight models that companies own and control is Rolnick's answer to the trap — avoiding platform dependence by keeping the core model layer outside any single lab's pricing power.

**[Robots Will Be Trained on Video Games - Pim de Witte, General Intuition](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MpkARD5pq-M)**
Pim de Witte argues that robotics models require a fundamentally different architecture than language models — continuous frame-in, action-out rather than turn-by-turn — and that Medal's game recording library (~1 billion videos/year, 1.5+ trillion action tokens/month) is uniquely positioned to supply training data at the scale this paradigm requires.

- **Spatial-temporal action models need streaming architectures.** Unlike LLMs that process and respond in turns, models for agents in physical or simulated space must ingest frames and emit actions continuously — the architecture difference is fundamental, not an incremental tweak.
- **Game controllers bridge directly to robotics.** Because standard game controllers also run most robots, training data from gaming generalizes to robot control in a way that most other video data does not, turning Medal's existing library into robotics training data.
- **Scale of game data is massive.** Medal captures roughly a billion game clip videos per year and generates over 1.5 trillion action tokens per month, providing a training data moat that purpose-built robotics labs cannot easily replicate.
- **One model, two application domains.** The same model architecture that plays games at a high level can control physical robots — the game-to-robot transfer is a design goal of the architecture, not an incidental property.
