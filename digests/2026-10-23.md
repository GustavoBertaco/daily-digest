# 🤖 AI & Machine Learning

## AI Engineer

**[1,368 AI Failures and What They Teach About Evals — Vishal Srivastava & Tanmay Sah, New York Life](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mMw3arwQsL0)**
Vishal Srivastava and Tanmay Sah analyzed 1,368 real incidents from a public AI incident database to identify what failures in production actually reveal about how AI systems should be tested — a ground-up empirical approach to eval design.

- **A structured taxonomy covers ~98% of incidents.** Failures are sorted across three dimensions — technical strength, deployment controls, and business impact — and 13 sub-areas, giving practitioners a reusable framework for mapping their own risk surface.
- **Weak deployment controls are most closely tied to harm.** Of all failure types, gaps in human review and monitoring show the strongest correlation to actual harm outcomes, meaning process and oversight failures outrank model capability failures in real incidents.
- **Use-case ranking by stakes is a prerequisite.** The speakers show how to rank use cases by potential impact before deciding what to evaluate and how rigorously — the risk profile determines the eval budget.
- **The evaluator itself needs to be tested.** A key structural point: the evaluation system can fail, so eval design must include validating the evaluator, not only the model being assessed.

**[How to Test AI Agents When No Benchmark Exists — Nathanael Weill, Air Canada](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c_SPW__jF9w)**
Nathanael Weill's team at Air Canada builds AI agents for tasks so domain-specific that no public benchmark exists and domain experts have little time to grade outputs — so they inverted the test-generation approach entirely.

- **Start from the answer, work backward to the input.** By generating test inputs from a known answer, the team can verify whether the agent recovers the original answer automatically, removing the need for a human to assess correctness on every run.
- **Test frequency jumped from one expert-graded test per week to dozens of automated tests per day.** This shift was first proven on a RAG project and then applied to a supplier-contract review agent.
- **Five methods for building eval cases in a low-data domain.** The contract review agent demonstrates five distinct test-construction approaches for domains with scarce labeled data — a practical reference for teams facing the same constraint.

**[Why AI Writing Still Reads Like Slop — Louis-François Bouchard, Towards AI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_dTF3NpPzGo)**
Louis-François Bouchard of Towards AI names the specific failure modes that make AI-generated writing feel generic, then shows how his team built and ran a benchmark to measure whether a model can write in a specific human voice.

- **Six named failure modes.** Overused words, vague generalities, saying nothing, repetition, hallucination, and a voice that "sounds like everyone at once" — each is a specific, actionable signal for any writing evaluation.
- **The benchmark used 10 scripts, 5 model drafts each, 4 LLM judges, 9 scoring metrics, and 1,000 human A/B checks.** The methodology is described in enough detail to be directly replicable.
- **LLM judges favor their own model family.** The experiment exposed systematic judge bias — a concrete reason not to rely on a single-model eval pipeline and to either mix judge families or add human ground-truth verification.
- **Cost and practical recommendations were shared.** The session ends with what the benchmark actually cost and what the team recommends for practitioners building voice-specific writing evals.

**[The AI Content System Behind 200M Views — Sarah Chieng, Cerebras & Cova Lee, OpenAI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CFbnVpSsDFQ)**
Sarah Chieng and Cova Lee share the agent-powered production system behind a high-volume content operation, covering all six stages from ideation to publication and the specific tools and patterns used at each step.

- **Six-stage pipeline, fully described.** Ideation, scripting, recording, editing, repurposing, and publishing — each stage is at least partially automated or agent-assisted.
- **Swipe files and custom research tools feed the ideation stage.** The first stage uses structured inputs rather than ad-hoc prompting, establishing a repeatable top-of-funnel for content ideas.
- **Voice-based scripting and "script skills" formalize the writing stage.** Shot lists and design systems carry that structure forward into recording and editing.
- **Long-form video is cut into shorts via API and computer use.** The repurposing stage specifically uses computer-use APIs — a less common production pattern worth attention for teams managing cross-format publishing.

**[Does Code Quality Still Matter? It Depends on This — Max Kanat-Alexander, Capital One](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bU5gDVyu_28)**
Max Kanat-Alexander argues that code quality's relevance depends entirely on whether software is meant to last — and that AI is normalizing a new category of disposable software where the usual quality arguments do not apply.

- **Quality existed to serve two purposes: understanding systems and safely changing them.** These remain valid; what changed is that a growing share of software does not require either property.
- **Disposable software has a specific profile: low consequences, few dependents, near-zero replacement cost.** If software meets this profile, investing in quality is a misallocation of time and attention.
- **For durable software, the quality bar rises.** AI-generated code can accumulate hidden complexity faster than humans can track it, making rigor more important — not less — for anything that must persist.
- **The session covers how to deliberately move software from durable to disposable.** Making a system more disposable is itself a design choice, and Kanat-Alexander shows how to do it intentionally rather than by accident.
