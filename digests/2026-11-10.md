## Daniel Pink

**[The Person Beside You Knows Something You Don't!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G2lrFMR4JCg)**
Nicholas Epley's research at Chicago Booth challenges the widespread instinct to stay silent on commutes: people who struck up conversations with strangers reported more pleasant trips than those who kept to themselves, yet a separate group predicted the opposite would be true.

- **The prediction gap is consistent and directional.** People expect talking to strangers will make a commute worse; the actual experience reliably goes the other way.
- **Fear of rejection is largely unfounded.** In Epley and Schroeder's commuter train experiment, almost nobody who tried to start a conversation was turned down.
- **The error runs in one direction.** We underestimate both strangers' willingness to engage and the enjoyment we would get from brief social contact — a systematic misprediction, not random noise.

## Big Think

**[What is panpsychism? | Jack Symes](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=p4dAIspaKb8)**
Jack Symes, philosopher at Durham University and host of The Panpsycast podcast, explains panpsychism — the view that consciousness is a basic feature of the universe rather than an emergent product of complex matter.

- **Consciousness as fundamental, not derived.** Panpsychism rejects the standard framing in which subjective experience arises from non-experiential physical processes; instead it treats experience as a primitive feature of reality.
- **The hard problem motivates the view.** By making consciousness fundamental, panpsychism sidesteps the question of how experience could ever emerge from matter that has none — an open problem in mainstream philosophy of mind.
- **Symes brings an accessible platform.** His Panpsycast podcast is a dedicated venue for philosophy of mind, and this clip excerpts a fuller interview available on the Big Think channel.

**[Who you are fluctuates every single second | Lisa Feldman Barrett](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NEq_CU_weas)**
Psychologist Lisa Feldman Barrett argues that traits like "introvert," "extrovert," "dismissive," or "engaged" are not fixed internal properties but relational descriptions — accurate snapshots of a particular interaction, not stable facts about a person.

- **Traits describe relationships, not individuals.** Ask five people who you are and you get five equally valid answers; Barrett uses this to show that the self is not a singular, stable object carried inside one person.
- **Identity is contextual and moment-to-moment.** Because traits emerge from interactions, who someone appears to be shifts with each context and partner.
- **A challenge to dispositional thinking.** Judging someone from a single interaction, or attributing their behavior to character rather than circumstance, misreads what trait language actually captures.

Mislav Ivanda of Daytona, having integrated sandboxes into most major agent frameworks, argues that isolated sandboxed execution is the one design decision all frameworks converge on — regardless of their other architectural differences.

- **Three approaches, one survivor.** Allow lists with approval prompts, embedded runtimes, and separate sandboxed computers are compared; only the sandboxed computer prevents a compromised step from compromising the host application.
- **The convergence is empirical.** Frameworks that disagree on nearly everything else — CrewAI, AG2, the OpenAI Agents SDK — all ended up at external sandboxes.
- **Blast radius is the decisive criterion.** The key failure mode is not that agents run bad code; it is that bad code can escape the agent step and affect the broader application.
- **Sandboxes are becoming standard infrastructure.** Ivanda's framing treats the sandboxed computer as a necessary component of agent deployments, not an optional security add-on.
