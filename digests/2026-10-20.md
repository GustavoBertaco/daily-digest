## AI Engineer

**[The Model Swap Workshop: GPT-5.5 vs Kimi vs DeepSeek vs Mistral — Pamela Fox, Microsoft](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UGjk3Uk0MxM)**
Benchmarks tell you how a model performs in aggregate; they tell you nothing about how it behaves in your application. Pamela Fox, Principal Cloud Advocate at Microsoft, runs the same scenarios against GPT-5.5, Kimi K2.6, DeepSeek V4 Flash, and Mistral Large 3 on Microsoft Foundry to make those differences visible.

- **Swap and test, don't benchmark and guess.** Running your actual scenarios is the only reliable way to understand model behavior in your specific application — aggregate benchmarks are proxies, not predictors.
- **Four models on the same scenarios.** GPT-5.5, Kimi K2.6, DeepSeek V4 Flash, and Mistral Large 3 are tested head-to-head via Microsoft Foundry, making behavioral differences directly comparable.
- **Knowing where models diverge is the practitioner skill.** Fox structures the workshop to help teams anticipate which model characteristics matter for their workload before committing to a switch.

**[Why Buying Companies Beats Selling Them AI — Prathik Naidu, Long Lake](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=J7n3WJnFTto)**
Selling AI into established service businesses from the outside is hard: org charts are messy, trust takes decades to build, and service firms don't scale like software. Long Lake's thesis is that you have to own the business first, then transform it from the inside.

- **The vendor problem is structural.** External AI sellers face organizational friction, legacy trust dynamics, and service-firm economics that software-style go-to-market can't overcome.
- **Acquisition as the deployment channel.** Long Lake acquires established service businesses across residential services, architecture, HR, and more — using ownership to remove the political and structural barriers to AI adoption.
- **40 companies as the evidence base.** Naidu draws on a portfolio of 40 businesses to describe both the selection criteria for acquisition targets and what transformation actually looks like once they own it.

**[Version-Controlled Search: The Web as It Was — Ryan Ahern, Exa](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vS2IA9pq0Q4)**
As agents increasingly broker web searches, retrieval quality becomes a first-class engineering concern — Exa Snapshot addresses this by letting agents query the web as it existed on any specific past date.

- **Agents are the new searchers.** When agents intermediate most searches, retrieval quality, reliability, and time-anchoring matter more than UX considerations — the engineering requirements shift.
- **Snapshot vs. live search.** Exa Snapshot returns results from a specific historical point in time, enabling use cases where current-web content would introduce anachronistic or contaminating information.
- **Predictive signals without overfitting.** Point-in-time search lets teams build and backtest signals without inadvertently leaking information from after the prediction date.

**[Backtest Your Agent Against Yesterday's Web — Ryan Ahern, Exa](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=L4dUFimVDKU)**
Version-controlled search turns agent backtesting from a thought experiment into a practical workflow — Ryan Ahern demonstrates with a hands-on example: predicting company acquisitions from signals like leadership turnover, tuned on data that was knowable at prediction time and nothing more.

- **The backtesting gap.** Most agent evaluations test current behavior; validating whether an agent would have made the right call historically is a distinct and underused discipline.
- **Acquisition prediction as the worked example.** Leadership turnover signals are used to predict acquisitions, with the system tuned on historical data to avoid contamination from future information.
- **Version-controlled search enforces the time boundary.** Restricting retrieval to what was indexed on a specific past date is the mechanism that makes clean backtesting possible — no information leakage by construction.

**[Why AI Agents Still Won't Pay for Anything — Jan Curn, Apify](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-AOxpwVeIWw)**
Despite a proliferation of agent payment standards — x402, AP2, ACP, MPP, TAP, UCP — real transaction volume from AI agents is close to zero. Jan Curn, founder of Apify, maps the payment funnel to show exactly where it breaks down.

- **Standards proliferation without adoption.** Every major fintech and payments company now has an agent payment protocol; none has meaningful volume — a market structure problem, not a missing technical standard.
- **The funnel breaks at discovery and trust.** Walking through need, setup, discovery, and purchase, Curn identifies where friction accumulates for agents trying to pay autonomously.
- **API keys still win.** Despite the ecosystem of agent payment proposals, API keys dominate because they are simple and already integrated into existing tooling.
- **Crypto is structurally well-placed.** Curn identifies crypto as well-suited to the agent payment use case, covering why it is better positioned than the alternatives despite current low adoption.

**[Stop Telling Agents How — Dan Barrett, Raymond Weitekamp & Jose Montes de Oca, OpenProse](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7T-gldvgfpo)**
OpenProse's approach: write multi-agent workflows as declarative markdown contracts specifying what outcome you want — not how to get there — and let any harness (Claude Code, Codex, and others) compile and execute them.

- **Declarative over imperative.** Define the goal and constraints in plain markdown; the harness resolves the execution path. This separates what from how, and makes workflows readable without understanding the underlying execution model.
- **Portable across harnesses.** The same markdown contract runs on Claude Code, Codex, and other systems — portability that imperative scripts can't provide.
- **Concrete demos.** Event-driven jobs triggered by webhooks and pull requests, a "grumpy senior" PR reviewer, and an end-to-end bank model-documentation workflow are shown as working examples.
- **Composability and pinning.** Contracts can be composed; a successful agent run can be pinned into a reusable workflow, closing the loop from exploration to production artifact.

**[Insurance Claims Are AI's Messiest Data Problem — Denys Linkov, Wisedocs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W4pnzOURwHQ)**
Insurance claims — full of handwriting, faxes, duplicates, and 8GB PDFs — present one of AI's hardest document intelligence challenges; Wisedocs' Denys Linkov argues that the unstructured mess is simultaneously the biggest barrier and the biggest opportunity.

- **The data quality problem is the moat.** Whoever solves claims ingestion and cleaning owns a durable advantage — the messiness is precisely what keeps competitors out of the market.
- **Three layers of analysis.** Linkov frames the problem as: cleaning and ingestion, within-document analysis, and cross-claim insights — each requiring different technical approaches and evaluation criteria.
- **8GB PDFs are a real engineering constraint.** Document size is not a theoretical concern; Wisedocs built infrastructure specifically to handle claims that exceed what standard document loaders can process.
- **Purpose-built benchmarks reveal what general ones miss.** Wisedocs built a benchmark with Artificial Analysis specifically for medical records — the edge cases that matter in claims processing do not surface in general-purpose model evaluations.

**[Evals for High-Stakes AI Agents — Doneyli De Jesus & Annabell Schäfer, Langfuse](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=obfPzbapMIM)**
Langfuse walks through the full evaluation loop for AI applications where mistakes are expensive — tracing, dataset construction, LLM-as-judge scoring, and CI gates — using a banking assistant as the worked example throughout.

- **Domain experts generate the failure modes.** The process starts with domain expert comments, not engineer assumptions, converted into structured failure modes — grounding evals in the scenarios that actually matter in the deployment context.
- **Tracing with PII masking.** Observability in sensitive domains requires stripping personal data before logging; Langfuse treats this as a first-class step in the workflow, not an afterthought.
- **LLM-as-judge and code evaluators are complementary.** LLM judges handle subjective quality assessments; code evaluators handle verifiable correctness — both are used in the banking assistant example rather than choosing one approach.
- **CI blocks regressions automatically.** A prompt change that drops eval scores is blocked before reaching production — the gate is automated, removing the reliance on manual review to catch degradation.

**[Teach Your Agent to Prove It's Done — Nick Nisi & Zack Proser, WorkOS](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q4WC4TowkBo)**
Agents that declare completion without verification are one of the most common production failure modes — WorkOS demonstrates how to build verification loops so that a failed check drives the next fix automatically rather than surfacing to a human.

- **Verification closes the loop.** Give agents a mechanism to check their own work and failures become inputs to the next iteration — rather than bugs that surface on someone's desk.
- **A practical verification toolkit.** The workshop covers clear goal definition, CLI health checks, browser snapshots, LLM judges, and evals — each suited to different verification scenarios.
- **Failure as a trigger, not a terminal state.** The architecture routes failed checks back into the agent's context for a fix attempt, rather than surfacing the failure externally as a human task.
- **Live demo with a repo.** The "Midnight Snack Club" demo app comes with a public repo for following along with the implementation.

**[An AI Agent Got Its User Banned From Resy — Keycard, JPMorgan Chase & AAuth](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IavxrdVnwXM)**
A restaurant-booking agent made 200 API calls in 90 minutes and got its own user banned from Resy — a concrete illustration of why agent authorization frameworks remain inadequate, examined in a panel with Keycard, JPMorgan Chase, and OAuth co-creator Dick Hardt.

- **Good intentions, no limits.** The Resy incident anchors the panel: the agent was not malicious, it simply operated with no rate limits, permission scoping, or authorization boundaries.
- **OAuth scopes are too coarse for agents.** Current authorization primitives were designed for human-scale interactions; agents need fine-grained, revocable, time-bounded access that today's scope model cannot express.
- **Companies still won't hand agents real autonomy.** The panel examines what structural and technical changes are required before enterprises are willing to grant agents meaningful autonomous capability.
- **Graduated trust as the design pattern.** Hardt's framing: provision agents with limited permissions that expand as they demonstrate reliability — treating agents like junior employees whose access grows with demonstrated judgment.
