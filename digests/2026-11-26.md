## Daniel Pink

**[Negative Space Hidden Logos](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=d7CqCRlzFTA)**
Daniel Pink draws on a 1979 memory study to show that the most familiar objects are often the worst recalled — a cognitive gap that explains how negative space can hide a shape inside a logo that people see every day without truly observing.

- **Familiarity is not memory.** Nickerson and Adams (1979) asked American adults to draw a penny from memory; on average, participants correctly placed only about three of the coin's eight features.
- **Recognition and recall are separate capacities.** When shown fifteen penny variants, fewer than half of participants selected the real coin — despite having handled thousands of pennies over their lifetimes.
- **The logo connection is in the data.** The study was framed as testing something "more familiar than any logo," explicitly linking the penny's poor recall to how everyday marks are perceived without being encoded — the same perceptual mechanism negative-space logos exploit.

## Big Think

**[Why narcissistic abuse is hard to see | Ramani Durvasula](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X1bm60e_QUk)**
Clinical psychologist Ramani Durvasula argues that narcissistic abuse evades recognition because it accumulates through repeated small harms rather than a single dramatic event — making self-doubt, not clarity, the typical first response for the person inside the relationship.

- **"Death by a thousand cuts."** Durvasula's central framing: no individual incident crosses a recognizable threshold, so the pattern normalizes before it is named.
- **The recognition barrier is structural, not personal.** Because there is no single triggering event to point to, people in narcissistic relationships lack a natural anchor for identifying what is happening to them — which is part of why the harm persists.
- **The book thesis starts here.** The clip is drawn from a full Big Think interview with Durvasula about her book "It's Not You"; the death-by-a-thousand-cuts framing — why there is never a single visible event to point to — is the central problem she works from, not a footnote.

## AI Engineer

**[How 21 People Built a $100M+ Agent Company — Quinn Slack, Amp](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Xyb1Bn_L2U4)**
Quinn Slack describes how Amp, spun out of Sourcegraph with 21 people and unable to hire for a year, reached $100M+ in ARR by rebuilding its entire operating model around agents — eliminating task trackers, PMs, and large releases in favor of a minimal-meeting, agent-first workflow.

- **No task tracker, no PMs, one meeting a week.** With a hiring freeze forcing efficiency, Amp stripped out conventional process entirely, moving all coordination into the agent rather than adding management overhead.
- **Tiny monitored pushes over big releases.** The release strategy is incremental: small, observable deployments that agents can instrument and roll back, replacing a high-coordination release cycle.
- **A bug-report loop that closes itself.** Amp's agents fix a reported bug and automatically email every affected user, compressing the full support-to-resolution cycle without human handoffs.
- **Built their own CRM instead of using Salesforce.** At their scale and with an agent-first infrastructure, owning a custom CRM was more cost-effective than adapting a standard tool to their operating model.
- **$100M+ ARR with 21 people.** The snippet describes the company running some multiple more efficiently than conventional organizations — the specific ratio is cut off in the transcript excerpt, but the headline headcount-to-ARR ratio is itself the clearest illustration of what an agent-first operating model enables.
