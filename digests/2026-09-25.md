## The Pragmatic Engineer

**[Design Engineering with Maggie Appleton](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg)**
Maggie Appleton, a staff research engineer at GitHub Next, explores what software engineers can learn from design engineers — practitioners who sit close enough to code to build and close enough to users to understand how tools shape behavior. The episode draws on her work building prototypes for new human-AI collaboration patterns.

- **Design engineering is a cross-disciplinary practice, not a hybrid title.** Appleton works at the intersection of design, anthropology, and web development — a combination that makes her prototypes address how people actually use tools rather than how designers expect them to.
- **GitHub Next as a prototyping laboratory.** Her current role centers on exploring how software engineers might collaborate with AI in fundamentally new ways, with the lab structure enabling rapid build-and-discard cycles.
- **Anthropology changes what you notice.** A background in anthropology trains attention on actual use versus intended use — a lens that proves especially useful when designing human-AI interaction where observed behavior routinely diverges from design assumptions.
- **Being the first designer at an AI startup means building the function and the rationale for it simultaneously.** Appleton was the first designer hired at an AI startup, which placed the work of defining what design engineering contributes alongside doing the work itself.

# 🤖 AI & Machine Learning

## AI Engineer

**[Physical AI's Next Bottleneck Is Finding the Right Video — Rafael Levi, Bright Data](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I_VEh7XSwyc)**
Rafael Levi of Bright Data argues that the defining constraint for physical AI is not model architecture but data: LLMs train on trillions of words, while robotics has roughly one million videos of robots performing actions — orders of magnitude less.

- **Staged data is structurally biased.** When people are told to open a door naturally for a camera, they don't behave naturally. Teleoperation produces demonstration data, not authentic behavioral data.
- **The web already contains the physics.** Billions of hours of real humans handling objects — showing real gravity, motion, and cause and effect — already exist online. The problem is identifying and filtering it at scale.
- **Noise is the core engineering challenge.** NVIDIA discards approximately 96% of video data during training for physical AI, which means the cost of quality control at web scale is enormous but apparently worthwhile.
- **"AI without data is just a box."** Levi's framing positions data acquisition and curation as the primary leverage point for robotics progress, ahead of model architecture.

**[World Models Need Causality, Not Pretty Pixels — Christopher Manning, Moonlake AI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4Gqg0HVe-AY)**
Christopher Manning, Stanford professor and former Stanford AI Lab director, now at Moonlake AI, tours AI history from Dartmouth 1956 through Shakey the robot to present-day LLMs to make the case that the next frontier is embodied intelligence built on simulation — not larger language models.

- **Scale at the frontier is not new.** In 2007, Google already had a language model trained on 2 trillion tokens — the same order of magnitude as today's frontier models. Scale alone has not resolved the hard problems.
- **Each AI era had its own bottleneck.** Manning's historical tour is an argument that current limitations are structural, not a matter of needing more of the same ingredients.
- **Generative video as a simulation substrate.** Models like Genie 3 represent his proposed path forward — using generated video to simulate physical environments where causal structure can be learned rather than correlated away.
- **World models require causality, not pixel fidelity.** Visually convincing video does not encode why things happen; physical AI needs the causal why, not just the perceptual what.

**[Robotics Has Been Stuck for 70 Years — Deepak Pathak, Skild AI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jFHteJjRl8A)**
Deepak Pathak, co-founder and CEO of Skild AI and a Carnegie Mellon professor, argues that robotics stalled for seven decades because the field treated it as a hardware problem rather than a problem of building a general brain.

- **A 1957 demo looks like a 2026 demo.** Most observers cannot distinguish a robotics demonstration from 1957 from one made today — Pathak uses this as evidence that hardware changes did not move the capability ceiling.
- **The data math is damning.** At one demonstration per minute via teleoperation, engaging the entire US population would take more than a century to accumulate GPT-3-scale training data for robots.
- **Skild's approach: an omni-bodied brain.** Rather than training a separate brain per robot embodiment, Skild trains a single brain across many robot types — applying the foundation model logic to physical intelligence.

**[From Scratch to SOTA: Training a 3B State-Space Vision Model — Krishna Prasad Srinivasan, Sarvam](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T72nqdC92PM)**
Krishna Prasad Srinivasan describes Sarvam's 3-billion-parameter vision language model built for Indian languages — a market that frontier labs call fast growing, yet one where less than 1% of the Common Crawl corpus used to train those frontier models is in any Indian language.

- **The knowledge exists but was never digitized.** The gap is not a lack of Indian language content in the world; it is the absence of digitized versions that make it into large training corpora.
- **Small and capable.** At 3 billion parameters the model fits on a single GPU and reportedly outperforms document AI models one hundred times its size on relevant tasks.
- **State space model backbone, not transformer.** The architecture choice departs from the standard vision-language model approach, suggesting that state space models are becoming viable competitors for this class of task.
- **A one-GPU model is a deployment requirement, not just a technical preference.** The markets Sarvam serves make resource-constrained inference a product constraint, not an optimization.

**[From VLM/VLA's to Embodied Agents — Armen Aghajanyan, Perceptron AI](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZZcE0HeO-Hc)**
Armen Aghajanyan of Perceptron AI identifies a structural inefficiency in how embodied AI models train on video: one hour of video produces roughly one million visual tokens, but training loss lands on only about 2% of them because the only supervision signal is a sparse transcript or a handful of labeled frames.

- **Most visual tokens are wasted.** Treating a background wall with the same weight as a gripper tip or contact point is a fundamental mismatch between what the model needs to learn and what it is being asked to predict.
- **The perceptive objective.** Perceptron's approach trains the model to learn which visual elements will matter, rather than prescribing salient regions in advance — the model surfaces relevance rather than receiving it as a hardcoded prior.
- **"A humongous waste."** Aghajanyan's framing is direct: current VLM training on video is structurally misaligned with what embodied agents need to extract from that video, and the loss function is where that misalignment lives.
- **Context is the second problem.** Beyond the token waste issue, the snippet points to context length and representation as additional constraints the talk addresses for embodied agent training.

**[You're Not Thinking Big Enough: Rebuilding Food Systems with AI Agents — Cody Menefee, Firecrawl](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ztlXKfPCT3Q)**
Cody Menefee, who raised turkeys illegally in a Nashville backyard and nearly quit engineering to farm full-time, argues that AI agents could unlock regenerative agriculture at scale by solving the labor problem that keeps only 3% of cattle finishing on pasture — despite pasture-finishing being better for animal welfare, consumer health, and land.

- **The gap is labor, not intent.** Pasture-finishing requires intensive grazing management — moving cattle, splitting paddocks, monitoring pasture recovery — that does not scale with human labor at the economics of commodity beef.
- **Personal experience grounds the thesis.** Menefee drove ten turkeys to a processor on the roof of his Tesla, experiencing firsthand the friction between small-scale regenerative practice and economic viability.
- **AI agents as the labor multiplier.** The talk frames agents not as a productivity tool for knowledge work but as the mechanism that could make ecologically sound food production economically viable at scale.

**[The Best Models Still Reason Like Toddlers — Andrew Dai, Elorian](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A_I8mw8yfns)**
Andrew Dai, co-founder and CEO of Elorian, presents a set of reproducible visual reasoning failures in current frontier models — including Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini — and argues the gap between human and model spatial reasoning is large enough to disqualify any current system from AGI claims in the visual domain.

- **Pattern matching actively hurts on hard visual questions.** Models identify a partial chessboard and return 32 white squares because training encodes "chessboard = 32 squares" — not because they count the visible portion. The heuristic that makes them good at identifying objects is the same one that defeats precise visual reasoning.
- **Spatial grounding is absent.** Asked where Catan pieces are positioned on a board, frontier models reason at length but return wrong counts — they cannot reliably answer detailed positional questions about specific board states.
- **The failure compounds directly in robotics.** Models miss discrete events in robot manipulation video — a lifted lid, a turned handle — which is a direct failure mode for robot control, not an abstract benchmark concern.
- **These failures are easy to reproduce.** Dai's point is not that edge cases exist but that the failures are consistent and findable in minutes, which frames them as structural limitations rather than gaps to be patched by the next model generation.

**[From Ingestion to Agents: How AI Teams Build on Document Intelligence — Adit Abraham, Reducto](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0I07YAuF8xA)**
Adit Abraham of Reducto, having processed billions of PDFs, argues that the shift from RAG to agents has fundamentally changed what a bad document parse costs: in RAG a bad parse degraded one answer; in an agentic pipeline it compounds across every downstream step.

- **PDFs were built to print, not to reason over.** The format encodes meaning visually — merged cells, line charts, handwriting — in ways that break text extraction and require visual understanding to recover.
- **Frontier models score roughly 30% on PDF reasoning benchmarks.** Even the newest models perform poorly on structured document decisions, framing document intelligence as an active unsolved problem, not a commodity capability.
- **Bad parsing is a multiplier in agents, not a ceiling.** RAG architectures limited the damage of a bad parse to one retrieval result; agent pipelines propagate the error across every action that follows.
- **The practitioner's lesson: parsing quality is now an architectural decision.** Because ingestion errors compound, document parsing belongs in the design conversation for agentic systems, not as an afterthought in the data pipeline.
