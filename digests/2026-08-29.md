## The Data Engineering Show

**[AI Still Needs Humans to Maintain Data Platforms #dataengineering #dataanalytics #datascience](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TzqdFITwwHk)**
Xavier Gumara Rigol argues that autonomous agents, despite their capability, cannot yet take over the maintenance or improvement of existing data platforms — the complexity of brownfield systems makes human oversight a structural requirement, not a transitional phase.

- **Greenfield vs. brownfield is the deciding variable.** Building from scratch is where AI agents are genuinely feasible; retrofitting them into existing, complex data environments introduces challenges current agents are not equipped to handle on their own.
- **Most real platforms are brownfield.** The practical implication is that the scenario where AI agents work best — starting from nothing — is the exception, not the norm, across the industry.
- **Human oversight is structural, not transitional.** The argument is not that agent technology is immature but that current data systems were never designed with agent integration in mind, making oversight a durable requirement.

## AI Engineer

**[AI-Native Organisations Run on Skills: How to Structure and Scale Them — Imad Touil, QuantumBlack](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M05vON8i0aI)**
Imad Touil argues that skills — not models, workflows, or infrastructure — are where an organization's actual institutional know-how ends up living, and that most teams are failing at everything beyond the first step of building them.

- **The adoption ladder collapses fast.** Most engineers in the room had built a skill; fewer shared them with their team; almost none governed or maintained them across the organization — and that shrinking sequence is the core problem.
- **Skills are the only workflow primitive that carries knowledge.** Of the four elements inside a workflow — hooks, sub-agents, MCP servers, and skills — hooks fire on events, sub-agents mostly protect the context window, and almost nobody writes their own MCP servers, leaving skills as the substance.
- **Governance and sharing are the unsolved problems.** The organizational challenge is treating skills as a managed asset class rather than a pile of local scripts that individuals build and forget.

**[Your Code Has Bugs. Lean4 Has Proofs: Formal Verification for Engineers — Varun Pant, AWS](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lRa9sPaMyy4)**
Varun Pant makes the case that formal verification via Lean4 is crossing from academic curiosity to a practical answer for the correctness gap opened by AI coding agents generating pull request volume too high for probabilistic review methods to clear.

- **The scale argument is new.** Coding agents are producing hundreds to thousands of pull requests per week; a model grading code is probabilistic, and tests only cover sampled paths — neither provides the guarantee the volume now demands.
- **AI can write the proofs.** An AI rewrote zlib in Lean4, emitting 32,000 lines of proof — decomposing the work into lemmas, closing each with tactics, and assembling them into a single theorem verified by an independent kernel.
- **The checking model is fundamentally different.** A small independent kernel verifying a theorem is a different kind of guarantee than a test suite or a model grader: the only paths to wrong are a bug in the kernel or an underspecified theorem.
- **One number to remember:** 32,000 lines of Lean4 proof generated by an AI to fully verify a reimplementation of zlib.

**[How do you diffuse AI into the real world? — Varun Shenoy, Long Lake](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B0fjR3yaZFU)**
Long Lake acquires service businesses rather than selling software to them — making it the operator and absorber of any AI failure — and Varun Shenoy uses that position to argue that the gap between demo and scaled deployment is structural, explained by the same diffusion curve that separates the 1880s electrical demonstration from Ford's electrified assembly line.

- **Owning the downside changes what you build.** Long Lake has acquired 35 businesses across property management, architecture, and HR, plus taken private the world's largest corporate travel platform in a 6.3 billion dollar deal — when the AI fails, there is no customer to blame.
- **The electricity analogy is a timeline warning.** Electricity was demonstrated in the 1880s; Ford's electrified assembly line came decades later. Diffusion from demo to scaled deployment is measured in years, not quarters.
- **Software vendors don't face this.** The gap between what AI can do in isolation and what survives real operational integration is invisible to sellers because they can attribute failure to the customer's implementation.

**[How to Get Your Org to Adopt Coding Agents (Without Shipping Garbage) — Eyal Blum, Figma](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Bn0xro2ol8)**
At Figma, the engineers slowest to adopt coding agents are the most senior — they carry institutional codebase context nobody wrote down and see every failure mode first — and Eyal Blum's argument is that treating their resistance as a requirements document is more productive than trying to convince them.

- **Best engineers resist first.** Senior engineers see agent failure modes before anyone else because they are the ones holding the codebase together with unwritten institutional knowledge — their trust bar is the highest and the most justified.
- **Resistance is a requirements document.** Their complaints form an ordered list of exactly where verification is missing — treating it that way lets the platform team prioritize what to harden rather than what to pitch.
- **Convincing vs. enabling is the wrong frame.** Figma's approach was to hand skeptical engineers the roadmap for making agents safe in the codebase, making them the authors of adoption rather than its audience.

**[From AI-Assisted to AI-Native: Building a Frontier Development Team — Clare Liguori, AWS](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqlWNihgdjI)**
Amazon observed 50 teams for close to a year and found that 90% used the same coding assistant, yet deployment velocity to production split into two distributions — the divergence explained entirely by how teams changed their workflows, not which tool they used.

- **The tool is not the variable.** 90% of teams used the same coding assistant; the outcome gap was explained by deliberate workflow change, not product choice.
- **Two distributions, not one.** Half the teams saw under 3x improvement in deployment velocity to production; the other half saw a median of 4.5x with some exceeding 10x. Treating AI assistance as a uniform uplift conceals a bimodal result.
- **Sprinkling agents on existing workflows is the low-outcome path.** Teams near baseline kept their existing processes and layered agents on top; the high-outcome teams deliberately redesigned how they worked around AI capabilities.
- **One number to remember:** a median 4.5x improvement in deployment velocity to production among teams that changed how they worked — versus under 3x for those that did not.

**[How to avoid disaster when vibe-coding a billing engine — Andrew Garvin, Stripe](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mJqwmmOx4WA)**
Andrew Garvin demonstrates that a single sentence can produce a working billing sandbox, then explains why the invisible complexity of billing — auto recharge, credit expiry, overage — is exactly where vibe-coding without domain expertise causes disasters.

- **A sentence produces a working sandbox.** One prompt copying Lovable's pricing generated a customer, metered usage flowing in, and a draft invoice broken into separately scoped credit pools for builds, plan mode, cloud, and gateway calls.
- **The dangerous details are invisible.** Auto recharge, credit expiry, and overage handling are where billing engines cause real harm — they don't appear in the happy path and are not surfaced by a prompt.
- **Domain expertise is the multiplier, not the replacement.** Garvin cofounded Metronome, acquired by Stripe this year in its largest acquisition, so he knows where the model gets it wrong and can verify the output — vibe-coding is faster, but not self-auditing.
- **Credit pool scoping is the key design decision.** Separating usage into independently scoped pools (builds vs. cloud vs. gateway) is what makes a billing engine legible and auditable.

**[Productionizing LLM Gateways: Architecture, Tradeoffs and Hard Lessons — Kanish Manuja, Twilio](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zrZ1amZBSPw)**
Kanish Manuja frames the LLM gateway as a permanent fight between latency and reliability, with streaming as the central design decision that trades away fallback options in exchange for perceived speed — and "Something went wrong, please try again" as the honest accounting of that trade.

- **Streaming removes your fallback levers at failure time.** Once a response starts streaming, you have committed to that provider — tokens already sent cannot be recalled, so the fallback you built is unavailable exactly when you need it.
- **The error string is the cost of the trade.** "Something went wrong, please try again" is the output of a deliberate engineering decision: accept the visible failure rather than the latency cost of preserving optionality.
- **A gateway is a permanent frontier, not a solved problem.** The framing from Twilio's experience is that there is no stable optimum — every parameter setting is a point on a latency-reliability frontier.

**[AI Evals for Cross-Functional Teams — Nachiket Paranjape & Swaroop Chitlur Haridas, DoorDash](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bMjlRrWjdT0)**
DoorDash's GenAI platform team decentralized eval ownership by going API-first — stable endpoints let non-engineers point a coding agent at those APIs and vibe-code whatever annotation interface their specific use case requires, removing the platform team as the bottleneck for every eval workflow.

- **API-first is the organizational unlock.** Stable endpoints let strategy and operations staff build their own annotation tools — whether grading restaurant menus or reviewing images — without waiting for the platform team to build a UI for them.
- **Annotators don't need to be engineers.** Non-engineers handle DoorDash's eval annotation and build their own tooling; the platform team's job is to ship good APIs, not to anticipate every workflow.
- **Platform teams shouldn't own every UI.** Stopping UI anticipation and shipping stable APIs is the architectural choice that makes cross-functional eval ownership possible at scale.
- **Evals at scale require removing the engineering bottleneck.** Eval quality is limited by whoever controls the tooling — decentralizing that control is what gets you coverage across all use cases.

**[Building uReview, Uber's Multi-Agent Code Review Engine — Will Bond & Ameya Ketkar, Uber](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EL123UNokkI)**
As PR volume and size both grew at Uber, average first-review wait times went from 3 hours in 2024 to 9 hours in 2026, making code review the engineering bottleneck across thousands of engineers — and the reason Uber built uReview rather than buying a solution.

- **Review wait time is the metric that broke the system.** First-review wait tripled in two years — 3 hours in 2024 to 9 hours in 2026 — as both volume and PR size grew across thousands of engineers at 12 sites.
- **Build vs. buy was a Phabricator problem.** Most code review vendors do not support Phabricator, which forced a build decision and gave Uber full control over how agents integrate into the inner review loop.
- **Scale multiplies the constraint.** Six language-specific monorepos across hundreds of teams meant the review bottleneck was slowing the entire engineering organization, not just individual teams.
- **One number to remember:** average first-review wait grew from 3 hours to 9 hours between 2024 and 2026.

**[Building the Engine While Flying the Plane: Launching the Figma MCP Server — Jesse Lumarie, Figma](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZIYYsAzaLlA)**
Jesse Lumarie turned a one-day-a-week unofficial project into Figma's first MCP server and one of the company's fastest-growing products in about three months — built while the MCP spec itself was changing beneath the team.

- **Permission to start small is the key.** Without a formal 20%-time program, Lumarie carved out one day per week based on an internal demo, and the informal start is what made the three-month timeline possible.
- **The goal was access for non-designers.** The initial motivation was enabling engineers and other non-designers to pull assets directly from Figma, which framed the MCP interface around that use case from the start.
- **The spec moved while they were building.** Weeks in, the MCP protocol deprecated the transport they had chosen, forcing a mid-build pivot while client support across the ecosystem was still uneven.
- **Speed-to-market despite instability.** Despite the spec churn, the server became one of Figma's fastest-growing products — suggesting that early presence in a new ecosystem compounds in ways that later entrants don't capture.
