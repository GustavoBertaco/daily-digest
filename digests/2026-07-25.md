## AI Engineer

**[Training Frontier Models to Out-Think Hackers — Uri Rolls, Arithmetic & Thom Wolf, Hugging Face](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O-CBZ3JtRvo)**
Uri Rolls and Thom Wolf ran a live adversarial evaluation against a real privilege escalation vulnerability — a genuine zero-day embedded in a Keycloak/Vault/broker chain — to test whether frontier models could discover and exploit it, finding that the best models probe the attack surface thoroughly but stop short of synthesizing an exploit.

- **A genuine zero-day, not a contrived CTF challenge.** The vulnerability involves a naming vs. ID inconsistency: one admin check validates by username, another by user ID, allowing a low-privileged user to rename themselves to the admin account and inherit elevated privileges. It is present in the real system, not planted.
- **Frontier models probe but do not exploit.** GPT 5.5 and Opus both traverse the chain, reach the vulnerable check — but never make the logical connection that combining the two inconsistent checks enables privilege escalation.
- **The gap between probing and exploiting is the security-relevant signal.** Models capable of thorough reconnaissance that still cannot synthesize an exploit represent a distinct and bounded threat level — different from models that fail to probe at all, and different again from models that successfully exploit.

**[The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Separating the Task from the Model — Maxime Rivest & Isaac Miller](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GgLQ02aO-hs)**
Maxime Rivest and Isaac Miller make the case for DSPy's Signature abstraction as a durable AI engineering pattern: by declaring a task only through its inputs and outputs — without committing to any specific model, prompt template, or provider API — teams preserve the freedom to swap implementations entirely without touching the task definition.

- **The Signature is the stable abstraction.** A task declared strictly through its inputs and outputs remains correct even as models deprecate, APIs change shape, and new provider options emerge — the specification does not drift with the implementation.
- **Decoupling unlocks ongoing experimentation.** Once the task shape is fixed, teams can independently experiment with models, prompt templates, fine-tuned weights, and provider APIs — a clean separation of concerns that keeps the task definition out of provider-specific churn.
- **The pattern survives the transition to tools, RLMs, and Skills.** DSPy's Signature is framed as useful not just for simple prompt-response tasks but in a world of tool-calling, reinforced language models, and composable Skills — it is not a pattern that becomes obsolete as AI engineering evolves.
- **The payoff is flexibility over the product lifecycle, not simplicity today.** The argument is not that DSPy reduces upfront complexity, but that it keeps the underlying execution layer interchangeable across the full lifetime of an AI-integrated product.

**[Notion's Token Town — Sarah Sachs, Notion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-I5W5QVAT8E)**
Sarah Sachs, who leads AI engineering at Notion and negotiates its model contracts, argues that LLM vendor economics are structurally adversarial: the same price-per-token can triple actual spend after a model upgrade, deprecation cycles force expensive migrations on the vendor's timeline, and buying tokens from a lab that also competes with your product is a conflict of interest that should shape every contract.

- **The unit price is not the cost.** A reasoning model upgraded at the same price per token can burn three times the output tokens; a 40% price increase on a new version compounds with forced migration when the predecessor is deprecated in four months.
- **Treating vendors as competitors is a deliberate strategic posture.** Sachs argues that a lab selling tokens while also shipping a first-party product is structurally a competitor — and that this should be the baseline assumption in negotiations, not a grudging discovery after pricing pressure appears.
- **Deprecation timelines are the hidden leverage point.** Four-month deprecation windows transfer switching costs entirely onto customers, forcing upgrades on the vendor's schedule rather than the product team's.
- **Revenue growth must pace model cost increases for margins to hold.** The framing of "are you growing 40%?" makes explicit that absorbing compounding price and token-burn increases requires revenue growth to match — a constraint many AI-integrated products cannot guarantee.

**[Harness Engineering is not Enough: Why Software Factories Fail — Dex Horthy, HumanLayer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ib5GBkD555M)**
Dex Horthy describes shutting down an agent software factory in July 2025 — a system where code was continuously generated but no human was actively reading it — and argues the failure was not a tooling or scale problem but a model training misalignment: coding models are reinforced on test passage, not on producing code humans can understand and maintain.

- **Nobody reading the code is not a workflow optimization — it is a failure mode waiting to trigger.** The factory ran unattended for roughly three months before an incident hit; at that point the codebase was opaque, prompting could not recover the system, and the site was down.
- **No harness, orchestration layer, or token budget fixes a training problem.** The claim is that the failure is upstream of tooling: coding models learn to satisfy test harnesses, and no amount of scaffolding on top of that training objective changes what the model optimizes for.
- **Coding models are reinforced on the wrong signal.** Reinforcement on whether tests pass — without a signal for human readability or maintainability — produces code that satisfies the harness while drifting toward something no human can safely intervene in when a real incident occurs.
- **Human oversight has a shelf life before entropy becomes unmanageable.** The failure did not appear immediately; it emerged after three months of disengagement. The implication is that codebase legibility degrades in the absence of active human reading, and the degradation compounds silently until a forcing event.

**[Perception Agents — Antje Barth, Amazon AGI Lab](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2JX6JYyQG4Y)**
Antje Barth frames perception agents as the missing architectural layer between agents that demo convincingly in controlled environments and agents that genuinely work alongside humans in real software — the gap being that most deployed agents depend on text descriptions of visual state rather than directly observing what is on the screen.

- **Current agents are blind to the screen.** Most production agents require a user to type a description of what they are looking at; they cannot navigate a UI that changes between interactions and fail when an application throws an unexpected modal or state change.
- **The demo-to-production gap is architectural, not a tuning problem.** Agents that perform well in demos fail alongside real teams in real software — the gap is architectural, not a matter of tuning or scale.
- **Perception agents see and act the way people do.** The architectural shift is giving agents direct visual observation of screen state — allowing them to reason about what they see and act on it, rather than depending on a human intermediary to translate the visual context into text.
- **Human-agent collaboration is becoming more visual as a modality.** The broader trend Barth identifies is a shift in how collaboration works: visual perception is moving from a nice-to-have to a requirement for agents to be genuinely useful in real work environments alongside human teammates.
