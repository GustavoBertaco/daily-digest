# 🤖 AI & Machine Learning

## AI Engineer

**[Improving Agents is a Data Mining Problem — Vivek Trivedy, LangChain](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CvRngaQZQ3Y)**
Vivek Trivedy's thesis is that agent observability and continual learning are the same problem — you cannot tell from code whether an agent degrades after context compaction, only from traces, and there are far too many traces to read yourself. The answer is pointing agents at other agents' traces to do the reading.

- **Observability and continual learning collapse into one problem.** Reading traces is the only way to know if your agent gets dumber after the first compaction, or the second. That's a data mining problem, not a monitoring one.
- **Trace-mining via other agents.** LangChain points agents at other agents' traces to surface degradation patterns, user frustration signals, and counterfactuals (what would a different model have done at the same step?).
- **The compaction problem is under-studied.** Most agent evals test fresh context; the degradation that accumulates across compaction cycles isn't visible until you look at trace sequences, not individual runs.

**[Lessons from Studying Every Memory System — Shlok Khemani, Independent](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5ZGyKWjQDr0)**
Khemani spent a year studying agent memory systems after discovering ChatGPT's profile for him contained a fabricated travel record — not just wrong, but incurious: the evidence to correct it was sitting in his email and nothing went looking. He frames this as a product problem, not a technology one.

- **Incuriosity is the real failure mode, not hallucination.** The system saw overlapping conversation dates (Turkey vs Thailand) and kept both — but more importantly, it never checked the email evidence that would resolve the conflict. A system that doesn't look for correction signals will stay wrong.
- **Conflicting records are stored rather than resolved.** The profile kept Turkey and Thailand simultaneously with overlapping dates — the system noticed no contradiction and made no attempt to reconcile them.
- **Product framing over technology framing.** Khemani's case is that the technology exists to build correct memory; the product decisions (what to store, when to verify, how to surface uncertainty) are what's missing.

**[Designing Agents (The Floor Is the Frontier) — Ben Hylak, Raindrop](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jHMiYtjoJfA)**
Ben Hylak's argument is that eval advice is still written for the chatbot era — when you knew the answer to nearly every question a user would ask. For agents, the useful question isn't what issues your agent has (it has effectively infinite issues) but which ones matter, and that turns on the gap between the ceiling and the floor.

- **Chatbot-era eval frameworks break for agents.** An 80% loss of signal after switching harnesses means your eval suite was measuring the harness, not the agent. The right question is which failures matter, not which failures exist.
- **Ceiling vs. floor framing.** The ceiling is the most impressive thing your agent can do; the question of which failures matter turns on the gap between that ceiling and the minimum bar users need to keep using the agent.

**[Bringing Continual Learning into Enterprises — Samuel Denton, Applied Compute](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZTA0GwpAUak)**
Applied Compute got a Qwen thinking model's SWE bench tool submission rate from 22% to 60% — with test pass rate held flat — by addressing a training data problem: the rollout was conditioned on production traces that never called the submit tool, so the teacher never touched those tokens. The fix moved the reasoning path toward the call rather than the call itself.

- **Training data poisoning through omission.** When the teacher trace set never includes a behavior, you cannot reinforce it directly. The fix was to shift the reasoning trajectory toward the tool call rather than supervising the call token itself.
- **A two-axis grid frames the problem space.** Denton maps continual learning interventions on a two-axis grid; enterprise use cases sit in a different region of that space than the pretraining-scale problems that shaped most existing techniques.
- **22% → 60% submit rate with flat test pass rate.** The improvement in task completion didn't come at the cost of accuracy — the model learned to conclude, not just to hedge. That's the key enterprise value: agents that finish work.

**[LLM Knowledge Bases: a practical guide — Ben Holmes, Warp](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I3bpdgFJCUY)**
Ben Holmes starts a knowledge base with voice dictation rather than organization — talking runs at ~200 words per minute and typing doesn't — and lets agents impose structure afterward. The capture step only works if there is enough raw material, so the step is deliberately sloppy.

- **Capture first, organize never (yourself).** The failure mode of most knowledge systems is optimizing the wrong step — investing in structure before there is enough content to structure. Holmes' advice: ramble into your phone after a meeting or podcast, skip the formatting, let agents handle organization.
- **Local, on-device capture tools remove friction.** He points to Handy and Voice Ink as options that don't require subscriptions or cloud upload — removing privacy and friction barriers to the capture habit.
- **Agents impose structure after the fact.** The downstream pipeline processes raw voice output into structured knowledge, meaning the human's only job is to say things — not to categorize or tag.

**[Adaption Labs: Gradient-Free Continual Learning — Sara Hooker, Adaption](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XEd_SRVHBgU)**
Sara Hooker estimates fewer than 5,000 people in the world know how to train a frontier model at scale, and that knowledge travels like an apprenticeship rather than a literature. She calls this the unreasonably narrow path — the right PhD, the right lab, the right moment — and argues it compounds the field's concentration problem.

- **Frontier training knowledge is apprenticeship-transmitted.** You cannot read your way to knowing how to train at scale — you need to have done it alongside people who have done it. This is a structural constraint on how fast the contributor base can grow.
- **5,000 people, 77 years of computer science.** In two generations of modern computing the contributor base for frontier training has narrowed into a single funnel. Hooker frames this as a compounding problem, not a temporary one.
- **Gradient-free updates as the alternative entry point.** Adaption Labs' gradient-free approach to continual learning is framed as a path to model improvement that does not require access to the narrow frontier training pipeline Hooker describes.

**[Intelligence + Continual Learning = Expertise — Yu Su, NeoCognition](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I6aiEf3aEFQ)**
Yu Su separates two concepts the field keeps collapsing: intelligence (reasoning through an unfamiliar problem from the context you were handed, where each episode stands alone) versus expertise (accumulated, domain-specific knowledge that makes a problem trivially easy on first glance). Scheduling a meeting isn't a slot-finding problem — it's a constraint optimization over authority, priority, and urgency, and an expert sees that immediately.

- **Intelligence and expertise are different axes.** Frontier models keep improving at intelligence (episode-level reasoning) while expertise (accumulated domain knowledge that persists across episodes) remains absent. Most enterprise value is in expertise, not intelligence.
- **The meeting example is load-bearing.** Finding a shared calendar slot is intelligence. Knowing how authority, priority, and urgency shape the actual scheduling constraint — and seeing that on first glance — is expertise. A very capable model does not.
- **Continual learning bridges intelligence and expertise.** The talk's central equation — Intelligence + Continual Learning = Expertise — frames continual learning as the missing term between what frontier models already do well and what makes a practitioner expert.

**[Scaling Compute on Context — Jack Morris, Engram](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WiqDvX6isc4)**
Jack Morris trains a model directly on 10,000 financial reports, drives loss to 0.00001, then generates from it — and the model collapses. The failure sets up his real argument: the three axes that powered the deep learning revolution (data, compute, model size) all run on public data. Your emails, meetings, and company data are outside that distribution, and that's where the value actually is.

- **Memorization ≠ generation.** Training to near-zero loss on a corpus means the model memorized it; it doesn't mean the model can generate useful outputs from that knowledge. Perplexity looks good right up until generation breaks.
- **Private data is out-of-distribution by construction.** Every frontier model is overfit to Wikipedia, arXiv, and GitHub. Your company's internal knowledge was never in the training set; retrieval and in-context approaches are compensating for a structural limitation, not solving it.
- **The next scaling axis is private data.** More compute and more parameters help on public benchmarks; they don't help when the relevant knowledge was never in the pretraining corpus. Morris' frame: scale compute on context rather than pretraining.

**[Memory Harnesses for Long-Running Research Agents — Stefania Druga, Sakana.ai](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=R3-anFK1YM8)**
On a literature review task where every paper fit inside the context window, adding a memory harness changed nothing — same accuracy, higher cost. That negative result marks the boundary: move to a long-horizon task where the answer is at step 124 and the question arrives at step 500, and the harness becomes the entire game.

- **Memory harnesses have a clear activation threshold.** They add value only when the relevant information falls outside the context window across the task horizon. Below that threshold, they add cost and complexity with no accuracy gain.
- **Write-manage-read is the control loop.** Druga frames memory as three steps: write (what gets stored), manage (how the store is maintained and pruned), read (when and how the agent retrieves). Most implementations optimize write and read; management — knowing what to forget — is the hard part.
- **The negative result is the most useful finding.** Knowing that a memory harness doesn't help on short-horizon tasks is as valuable as knowing it helps on long-horizon ones — it gives you a criterion for when to add the complexity.

**[Scaling up Continual Learning — Ronak Malde, Trajectory](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zL1kLftVTlo)**
Scale on-policy self-distillation to trajectories with 100 tool calls and the model collapses into hedging — Ronak Malde calls it the "but wait" problem: every token the model learns to favor fills up with "wait," "but," and "maybe," because the student drifts so far on a long task that the teacher course-corrects at every step, parking the model between two divergent distributions.

- **The "but wait" problem is a distribution collapse.** When the student drifts enough that the teacher intervenes at every step, the model learns the hedging signal rather than the task signal — it becomes expert at sounding uncertain rather than at completing work.
- **Long trajectories break standard distillation.** Techniques that work for short tasks (single tool calls, a few steps) stop working at 100-step trajectories because the student's distribution diverges too far from the teacher's before the task completes.
- **Trajectory-aware training is the implied fix.** The algorithm needs to account for cumulative drift over long tasks, not just step-level correctness — a different training objective than standard on-policy distillation.
